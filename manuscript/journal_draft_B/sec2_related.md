**Vietnamese captioning systems.** Published KTVIC results come from transformer captioners; GRIT (Nguyen et al., 2022) is the reference point most often cited for KTVIC. ViG (Vu et al., 2026) adapts transformer captioners to low-resource Vietnamese with grounding. **Reference-free scoring.** CLIPScore (Hessel et al., 2021) scores a caption against its image with an image–text model and needs no references. For Vietnamese it would need a Vietnamese image–text model. ViCLIP-OT (Tran et al., 2026) is one. It is trained on UIT-OpenViIC, and its authors reported that UIT-OpenViIC training images near-duplicate 401 of the 558 KTVIC test images; they evaluate on KTVIC only after removing these images (Section 3.6)<!-- src: arXiv 2602.22678v1 §5.1 (full text read 2026-10-29, pdf sha256 a4b41ef401a1c73c): "401 out of 558 test images and 2,464 out of 3,769 training images are identified as near-duplicates" (SSCD, Appendix B); LOG CC-163 W6 -->. Scores it gives on KTVIC test are therefore at risk of leakage, and we never use it as a metric or evaluator on KTVIC test.

**Vision–language models.** Vietnamese VLMs such as Vintern-1B (Doan et al., 2024) and general VLMs such as Qwen2.5-VL (Bai, Chen, et al., 2025) write fluent Vietnamese captions zero-shot.<!-- CC-145 J6: sentence cut (Section 4.1 cites QLoRA and LoRA). --> The supplement also uses Qwen3-VL (Bai, Cai, et al., 2025) and InternVL3 (Zhu et al., 2025).

**Reference-based metrics and their reporting.** CIDEr (Vedantam et al., 2015) weights n-grams by inverse document frequency over the reference set and divides by the norm of the n-gram vectors. Section 3.3 shows that this division turns an n-gram with zero document frequency, such as one containing a period the references never use, into a pure penalty. Caglayan et al. (2020) show other failure cases of the standard metrics, including high scores for degenerate system outputs. For machine translation, Jwalapuram (2023) shows that inserting or deleting a single sentence-final punctuation mark makes both string-based and model-based metrics produce significantly different scores.<!-- cite: jwalapuram2023pulling, DOI 10.18653/v1/2023.findings-acl.381, checked 2026-10-25 on api.crossref.org (title, single author, Findings of ACL 2023, pp. 6116-6130); wording per abstract, no number used --> sacreBLEU (Post, 2018) made BLEU's tokenisation and reference parameters reportable through a version signature. SacreEOS (Hu et al., 2023) did the same for the end-of-sequence convention in self-critical captioning training. Our protocol card (Section 4.2) applies the same idea to Vietnamese captioning: it names the segmenter (RDRSegmenter in VnCoreNLP; Nguyen et al., 2018; Vu et al., 2018), the CIDEr variant, the scale and the punctuation policy.

**Benchmark validity and dataset documentation.** Our audit belongs to work that asks what benchmarks measure (Bowman and Dahl, 2021; Raji et al., 2021) and that asks datasets to document how they were built (Bender and Friedman, 2018; Gebru et al., 2018); the protocol card is the evaluation-side counterpart. Kilickaya et al. (2016) re-evaluated automatic caption metrics against human judgments. The closest analogue of our image-overlap audit (Section 3.6) is the train–test overlap audit of open-domain question-answering datasets by Lewis et al. (2020).<!-- R2 section 4 (verified gaps); identifiers checked 2026-10-21 (Crossref, arXiv API). -->

**Object hallucination and its measurement.** CHAIR (Rohrbach et al., 2018) counts mentioned objects missing from a gold set built from instance labels and references. POPE (Li et al., 2023) probes objects with yes/no questions. Both rely on a lexicon that maps words to classes. For Vietnamese that lexicon has to handle tone-mark placement, classifier nouns and role nouns; Section 3.5 documents three versions of ours. The companion paper (in preparation) studies how the completeness of the gold and the number of mentioned objects confound these metrics, on English and Vietnamese sets (Section 7.3 lists which). We use its count-matched controls only as tools, in the supplement.

**Independence of references.** Caption metrics treat the references of an image as independent samples of how people describe it. Chen et al. (2015) model human caption agreement on that basis. Rater-equivalence analyses (Resnick et al., 2021) assume independent raters. When a few annotators write most of a dataset, models learn to recognise them, and performance on examples from unseen annotators drops (Geva et al., 2019). Who writes a description also changes its content: across 31 languages, including Vietnamese, describers from closer languages mention more of the same entities (Berger and Ponti, 2025). Reference quality matters for caption evaluation in its own right (Kasai et al., 2022).<!-- CC-145 J6: sentence "Two independent caption panels for the same image can therefore legitimately disagree." cut. --> KTVIC's single-writer design breaks the independence assumption by construction, and we measure how much (Section 3.2). The study closest to our two-panel comparison is Bromonschenkel et al. (2026). They train captioners on native and on machine-translated Brazilian Portuguese captions of the same Flickr30K images, and test each on the other set. Our two panels differ from theirs in two ways: both are written by people, under different protocols, and we measure how the gain from more training data differs between them (Sections 5.4–5.5), which they do not. Scaling studies of fine-tuning measure how performance grows with training data (Zhang et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2024), always against one reference set; we measure the same growth against two. **Second caption sets and reference style.** Separately written caption sets for the same images exist in other languages: Multi30K adds German descriptions of Flickr30K images (Elliott et al., 2016), and COCO-CN adds Chinese captions to COCO images (Li et al., 2019). Descriptions of the same images differ across languages (van Miltenburg et al., 2017), and native speakers' preferences between independently written and translated descriptions have been measured (Frank et al., 2018). The references themselves also move metric scores. Yi et al. (2020) make a caption metric account for the variance between an image's references; in machine translation, the quality of the references changes how well metrics agree with human judgments (Freitag et al., 2020); and captioners trained to match a dataset's reference distribution inherit its short, unspecific style (Kornblith et al., 2023). That training on one reference style raises scores against that style is therefore known qualitatively. Cross-domain captioning trains on one domain and captions another (Chen et al., 2017); a captioner fine-tuned away from mimicking its training references scores slightly lower against in-domain references but closer to the references of out-of-domain datasets (Dessì et al., 2023). In image classification, models lose accuracy under natural distribution shifts, and training on larger, more diverse data narrows but does not close that gap (Taori et al., 2020); across models, in-distribution and out-of-distribution accuracy are strongly correlated (Miller et al., 2021). What we add is narrow: the same images, two human caption panels, and a training-set-size slope measured against both, in both training directions, for Vietnamese.<!-- BD revision 2026-10-29 item 6 (R1 M4, R2 M6, R3 M7, R4 M7). **Preference and reinforcement tuning for captioning.** Self-critical sequence training (Rennie et al., 2017) optimises CIDEr directly, and reward-based methods add object or evidence rewards (Tang et al., 2026). The FAIR version (L.M.H. Nguyen et al., 2026) reported that 4-bit QLoRA DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) degraded its SFT adapter. The supplement (S6) reports such levers, including weight-space interpolation (Wortsman et al., 2022), as negative results only.
