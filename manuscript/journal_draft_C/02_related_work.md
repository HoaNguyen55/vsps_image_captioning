## 2. Related work

<!-- Citations are pandoc keys into paperC.bib (pass 3); they render in ACM author-year style. -->

**Retrieval for captioning.** Nearest-neighbour captioning borrows a caption from the most similar training image [@Devlin2015NN]. Our `nn_top1` and `nn_cons` baselines follow that idea and show how far copying alone would go. Later systems condition a trained decoder on retrieved captions [@Ramos2023EXTRA; @Ramos2023SmallCap]. Re-ViLM [@Yang2023ReViLM] adds retrieval from an external image-text database to a Flamingo-style model for zero- and few-shot captioning. Both of the closest such studies already contain a random-caption control. EXTRA [@Ramos2023EXTRA] trains and evaluates a variant that encodes the image with one random caption from the datastore in place of the retrieved ones, and that variant scores below the retrieval model. @Li2024RetrievalRobustness give SmallCap, at the same k, captions retrieved for another image, and its scores fall below those of the model without retrieval. A random draw from the same memory is therefore an established control for trained retrieval-augmented captioners. LMCap prompts a text-only language model with captions retrieved across languages and evaluates on XM3600 [@Ramos2023LMCap]. CONCAP [@Ibrahim2025CONCAP], also evaluated on XM3600, trains a multilingual captioner that adds image-specific concepts to the retrieved captions, to reduce its reliance on captions translated from English. MeaCap [@Zeng2024MeaCap] retrieves concepts from a textual memory, keeps those related to the image, and writes the caption with a keyword-to-sentence language model guided by CLIP, not with an MLLM. Case-based decision-theoretic decoding [@Deguchi2025CBDT] instead uses an in-domain memory to choose among sampled hypotheses, with the prompt unchanged. EVCap [@Li2024EVCap] retrieves object names from an external visual-name memory for a lightweight trained captioner, and ViECap [@Fei2023ViECap] decodes from entity names that CLIP recognises in the image, after training on text only. We instead show the image to the model, use an in-domain Vietnamese memory, and compare retrieval with random examples from the same memory.

The same study [@Li2024RetrievalRobustness] finds that retrieval-augmented captioners copy tokens from the retrieved captions and are sensitive to their order and selection. This motivates our copy diagnostics (4-gram copy rate, near-verbatim share) and our neighbour-only object analysis.

**Highest overlap.** The closest work is a training-free multilingual captioning study for remote sensing [@Rebelo2025RSCap]. It retrieves examples with SigLIP2, includes variants in which the model sees the image, and re-ranks the examples with PageRank across ten languages. Our study differs in four ways:
- everyday images in Vietnamese;
- random and fixed example controls at equal k;
- count-matched object precision;
- a preregistered one-look test.

Negative Entity Suppression [@Lu2025NES] also targets objects that retrieval brings in. It filters entities absent from the input out of retrieved captions and suppresses them at the attention level; Section 3.4 contrasts it with our post-decode step.

**In-context configurations in large multimodal models.** The closest English precedent is OpenFlamingo [@Awadalla2023OpenFlamingo], which uses retrieval-based in-context example selection (RICES) [@Yang2022PICa], as Flamingo also did [@Alayrac2022Flamingo]: example images are chosen by image similarity and shown, interleaved with their captions, at an equal number of shots. In its Appendix Table 9 (OpenFlamingo-9B), retrieved examples beat random ones on COCO by +4.1 CIDEr at 4 shots and +9.5 at 32 shots, while on Flickr-30K they did worse, by −20.3 at 0 shots and −13.6 at 4 shots. Our English COCO result (Section 8.2) is therefore a replication of a known English pattern, under different conditions: text-only example captions, an MLLM that sees only the query image, a frozen configuration chosen in another language, and a one-look test. The Flickr-30K reversal also shows that a model can score lower with retrieved than with random examples, as InternVL3 did on COCO (Section 8.2). @Yang2023ICLConfig compare image-selection and caption-assignment strategies for in-context captioning in English. @Li2025UnveilingICL analyse shot count, retrieval and attention inside such models. @dosSantos2025ContextVLM ask how much vision-language models use the images and texts placed in their context. @Baldassini2024MICL find that multimodal in-context learning in open interleaved models is driven mainly by the text of the examples, and that similarity-based example selection does no better than majority voting over the examples' answers; our `nn_cons` baseline, the consensus caption of the k nearest neighbours, is the captioning counterpart of that voting baseline. @Chen2025TrueMICL report that MLLMs tend to imitate the text of the demonstrations rather than use their visual content. Both findings are why the `rand` control is needed: a gain that comes from the example text alone, and not from its match to the image, appears in `rand` as well as in `ret`. GRIP [@Allabadi2026GRIP] trains an example retriever from model feedback. We train no retriever. Our examples are captions only, shown as text, and the configuration space (k, order, prompt tail, guard threshold) was searched only on development data.

**Vietnamese image captioning.** Public Vietnamese benchmarks include UIT-ViIC [@Lam2020UITViIC], KTVIC [@Pham2024KTVIC], UIT-OpenViIC [@Bui2023OpenViIC; @Bui2026OpenViICJournal], HDUCap [@Hoang2026HDUCap], whose 15,000 natural-scene images carry five human-written captions each and which comes with a spatially-aware trained transformer, and the Vietnamese references of XM3600 [@Thapliyal2022XM3600]. ViG [@Vu2026ViG] adapts a frozen English-pretrained captioner to Vietnamese by training lightweight grounding branches on Vietnamese data. Our study trains nothing: it compares text-only in-context examples against a random-example control. VELVET [@Vu2025VELVET] is a multitask, multilingual prompting vision-language model for Vietnamese, pretrained on image-text data translated from English. @Nguyen2026FAIR reduce hallucination in detailed Vietnamese captions by fine-tuning on supervision from verified propositions; the present study instead changes only the prompt and measures object precision as a registered non-inferiority check. Single-domain Vietnamese sets also exist, for healthcare (VieCap4H, from the VLSP 2021 challenge [@Nguyen2022VieCap4H]) and for scene text (ViTextCaps [@Nguyen2026ViTextCaps]); our study uses only general-domain sets. HDUCap is not among our test sets: our test sets were fixed in the registration, and its release page states no licence (GitHub, checked 2026-10-30). Beyond Vietnamese, AfriCaption [@Oduwole2025AfriCaption] builds captioning data and a model for 20 African languages. In a shared task on captioning in Indigenous languages of the Americas, @Dhawan2026Gators use retrieval-augmented many-shot prompting in a text-only translation stage that follows a Spanish caption from an MLLM; they report that retrieval helped only with large in-domain corpora. Our literature search was partial (details in the supplement, "Disclosure detail"). We make no priority claim and state only differences from the closest work.

**Object hallucination and its measurement.** CHAIR [@Rohrbach2018CHAIR] counts mentioned objects that the gold does not support. The companion object-precision study shows that object precision depends on how many objects a caption names, and defines the count-matched comparison we use as P2. On XM3600 we score verified precision against the released Open Images V7 human-verified labels [@Kuznetsova2020OpenImages], following that study's mapping rule.

Post-hoc checking with a second model is common [@Yin2023Woodpecker]. Our study registered such a guard, but its hypothesis was not passed at the development test and is reported only as a registered negative (Section 6.7).

**English benchmarks for the second arm.** The English arm uses the COCO Karpathy test [@Lin2014COCO; @Karpathy2015Split], scored with the standard PTB-tokenised CIDEr-D of the COCO caption toolkit [@Chen2015COCOCaptions], and NoCaps val [@Agrawal2019NoCaps]. NoCaps labels each image as in-domain, near-domain or out-domain relative to the COCO classes, which lets the English arm report the gain by distance from a COCO memory.

**What is new, stated against the closest work.** The random control itself is not new: EXTRA and Li et al. [@Ramos2023EXTRA; @Li2024RetrievalRobustness] use random captions from the same datastore with trained captioners, Li et al. at equal k. Studies of in-context selection for multimodal models compare similarity-based and random examples, mostly in English and with interleaved images [@Awadalla2023OpenFlamingo; @Yang2023ICLConfig; @Li2025UnveilingICL]. The count-matched object-precision rule comes from the companion object-precision study, and preregistration with one look is a reporting practice, not a contribution of this paper. What this study adds is narrower:
1. **The Vietnamese setting.** The retrieval-against-random comparison is run for Vietnamese captioning with training-free MLLMs, text-only example captions and an in-domain Vietnamese memory. The equal-k random control splits any gain over zero-shot prompting into a retrieval part and an in-domain-style part.
2. **The English comparison under one protocol.** The same arms, frozen configurations and decision rule are repeated in English with no re-tuning, on an in-domain and an out-of-source set, and reported within each language. On COCO this replicates the OpenFlamingo pattern under text-only examples and a one-look test; it is not a new English finding.
3. **Per-backbone reporting.** Every row is split by backbone. A development-only ablation (Section 6.8) is a non-confirmatory diagnostic; it does not establish a backbone account and does not carry beyond in-domain Vietnamese (Section 8.2).

The closest single study [@Rebelo2025RSCap] shares the training-free multilingual setting but has neither the equal-k random control nor a registered test. We make no priority claim on retrieval in-context captioning or on the random control.
