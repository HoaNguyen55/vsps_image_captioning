## Paper card

**Problem and stated limitation of prior work.** LLMs are being tried for automated essay scoring (AES), and an earlier study found that scored examples in the prompt help. The paper states that earlier AES studies give no guidance on which examples to choose or in what order, that the majority-label and recency biases known from sentiment classification are untested in AES, and that it is unknown whether such effects hold across minor versions of a model.

**Main claim.** The choice and order of in-prompt examples change the agreement between model and human scores, more for GPT-3.5 than for GPT-4; both biases are present; with well-chosen examples a GPT-3.5 version can exceed some GPT-4 versions; an older GPT-4 version is the most stable and accurate.

**Method / resource.** A measurement study, no new model. Prompts contain a rubric and zero to three scored example essays. Every ordered sequence of example labels (high, medium, low) is used: 3 one-shot, 9 two-shot and 27 three-shot categories, each with three randomly drawn example sets disjoint from the evaluated essays, so 117 few-shot prompts plus one zero-shot prompt (the abstract says 119 prompts, the method section 118). Six models score every evaluated essay under every prompt: 50,976 ratings. Agreement with expert ratings is measured by quadratic weighted kappa (QWK). Bias is estimated by linear regression on the three-shot prompts, with standardised predictors: the number of examples of each label, and the label of the last example. Coefficients are tested against zero (t-tests, level 0.05, Holm correction), once for the model's score (per essay and model) and once for QWK (per model).

**Theorem, bound, identification result or provable property.** None.

**Data and splits (sizes).** TOEFL11: 12,100 essays by learners of English on eight prompts, expert ratings on three levels. Evaluated sample: 72 essays (three per prompt and level). A pilot on 200 essays fixed the numeric values given to example labels. No training.

**Models and model families.** Three dated versions of GPT-3.5-turbo and three of GPT-4, one vendor; temperature 0.

**Baselines and their years.** The zero-shot prompt is the only baseline. No other example-selection strategy (for instance similarity-based retrieval of examples) and no non-LLM scorer is run. Closest works named: a 2023 study of essay rating with GPT-4 and a 2021 study of few-shot bias.

**Key numbers (as printed).** Score regression, "last example high": +0.0123, +0.0136, +0.0150 for the three GPT-3.5 versions (all p<0.001), +0.0020 (p<0.05) for the oldest GPT-4 version, not significant for the other two. "Last example low": −0.0136, −0.0173, −0.0207 (p<0.001) for GPT-3.5 and −0.0035 (p<0.01) for the oldest GPT-4 version. Mean R² of the score regressions: 0.14 to 0.30. QWK regressions: R² 0.5165, 0.3173, 0.3321 (GPT-3.5) and 0.3144, 0.0764, 0.1665 (GPT-4); "last example low" is +0.0240 (p<0.001) for one GPT-3.5 version and −0.0189 (p<0.05) for the next. QWK values are shown only in a figure.

**Ablations, seeds, statistical tests, confidence intervals.** Holm-corrected t-tests as above; three example sets per category; one response per prompt. No confidence intervals, no effect-size threshold, and no test of the GPT-3.5 versus GPT-4 comparison or of the statement that GPT-3.5 can exceed GPT-4.

**Failure analysis.** The authors explain the opposite signs across versions by different zero-shot scoring tendencies and state that this does not explain every case. Low R² is read as evidence that other factors matter.

**Code/data release and licence statements.** The prompt template is printed. No release of code or outputs is stated. The corpus is an existing licensed one.

**Positioning against closest prior work.** Prose; no table.

**Out-of-domain or cross-language checks.** None: one corpus, English essays, one vendor's models.

**Stated limitations.** Small evaluated sample (72 essays); examples are distinguished only by score, not by content; only GPT models; one dataset.
