## 8.2 A count control is a confound control, not a score

The count-matched difference removes exactly the part of a precision gain that comes from naming fewer objects, whereas judges rate a caption as a whole and, on long captions, penalize naming more objects. That it tracks human judgments worse (Section 7.1) is therefore not evidence against it as a control, but it rules out using it as a quality score. The two-column report of Section 3.7 is a proposal, untested as a confirmatory claim, with deposited code (Section 3.6).<!-- rev2 (CC-181 W8): condensed; original paragraph moved verbatim to Supplementary S10 -->

## 8.3 Using the results: a checklist for authors and reviewers

Two direct uses follow. A reviewer who reads that a decoding method lowers CHAIR can ask for $M$ per system and one pre-declared count-matched difference, which in Section 3.3 separates a gain that reverses at matched count (OPERA on Shikra) from the two that survive. A builder of preference data can check the chosen-minus-rejected object count before training (Section 7.3).<!-- pass 3 (2026-10-28): use-case paragraph (scorecard: application, venue fit); restates Section 6.2 ('One gain reverses'; 'the same two remain under both') and Section 8.4 ('Four sets carry a strong count channel'; length-matched shares); no new number --><!-- rev2 (CC-181 W8): condensed; original paragraph moved verbatim to Supplementary S10 -->

1. **Name the gold and the matcher, and report $M$ for every system.**
2. **Do not compare F1 across verbosity regimes under incomplete gold** without locating the systems relative to the F1 optimum (Section 6).
3. **Pre-declare one count control,** calibrate it with a seed placebo, and show the others in a specification curve (Section 3.3).
4. **Vary references with random subsets and report the adjacency statistic** (Section 5).
5. **For preference data, report the chosen-minus-rejected object-count difference,** not only length (Section 7.3).

## 8.4 Object metrics as quality-assessment measures

An object-hallucination score is a reference-based quality measure of a visual description, and its validity depends on its reference set, the gold. González-Chávez et al. (2024) ask whether caption metrics measure what they should; for object-level hallucination scores the sign of the verbosity slope is a property of the gold, so one score can rank two systems in opposite orders under two golds (Section 6.1), and reporting the gold is part of reporting the measure. The decomposition of Section 4 applies to any object-, attribute- or relation-level score with reference-built gold, but where the sign changes is not predicted, and a score that removes the count confound and tracks human judgments better remains open.<!-- rev2 (CC-181 W8): condensed; original paragraph moved verbatim to Supplementary S10 --><!-- rev2 (CC-181 W8): condensed; original paragraph moved verbatim to Supplementary S10 -->

## 8.5 Conclusion

Object-level hallucination scores respond to the gold and to the number of objects a caption names, not only to object choice. In a registered out-of-sample test on XM3600, and with a second Qwen backbone on 537 of the same 600 images, F1's verbosity slope is ordered by gold completeness; its positive sign under human-verified labels holds only when unverifiable mentions are excluded, and on XM3600 the reference-gold signs rest on one verbose system (both post hoc). Nine registered predictions failed (Table 1), among them the label-gold part of the PASCAL-50S replication, the all-references sign on XM3600 and both count-matched human-agreement predictions; one gold-size value missed its interval, four secondary predictions were inconclusive and one PASCAL-50S companion prediction was not evaluable. No count-control claim is confirmatory.
