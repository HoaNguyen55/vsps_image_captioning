## 8.2 A count control is a confound control, not a score

The count-matched column measures a different quantity from what human judges rate. Judges rate a caption as a whole and, on long captions, penalize naming more objects; the count-matched difference removes exactly the part of a precision gain that comes from naming fewer objects. That it tracks human judgments worse (Section 7.1) is therefore not evidence against it as a control, but it rules out using it as a quality score. We offer the two-column report of Section 3.7 as a proposal, untested as a confirmatory claim, and deposit the code that computes it (Section 3.6) so that others can test it on their own comparisons.

## 8.3 Using the results: a checklist for authors and reviewers

Two direct uses follow. A reviewer who reads that a decoding method lowers CHAIR can ask for $M$ per system and one pre-declared count-matched difference; in the post hoc re-scoring of Section 3.3 this separates a gain that reverses at matched count (OPERA on Shikra) from the two that survive both order-preserving controls. A builder of preference data can check the chosen-minus-rejected object count before training, since four of seven public sets carry a count channel that length matching does not remove (Section 7.3).<!-- pass 3 (2026-10-28): use-case paragraph (scorecard: application, venue fit); restates Section 6.2 ('One gain reverses'; 'the same two remain under both') and Section 8.4 ('Four sets carry a strong count channel'; length-matched shares); no new number -->

1. **Name the gold and the matcher, and report $M$ for every system.**
2. **Do not compare F1 across verbosity regimes under incomplete gold** without locating the systems relative to the F1 optimum (Section 6).
3. **Pre-declare one count control,** calibrate it with a seed placebo, and show the others in a specification curve (Section 3.3).
4. **Vary references with random subsets and report the adjacency statistic** (Section 5).
5. **For preference data, report the chosen-minus-rejected object-count difference,** not only length (Section 7.3).

## 8.4 Object metrics as quality-assessment measures

For readers of image-quality and caption-metric work, the result has a familiar form: a quality measure's validity depends on its reference set. González-Chávez et al. (2024) ask whether caption metrics measure what they should; we ask the same of object-level hallucination scores and find that the sign of their verbosity slope is a property of the gold. Because one score can rank two systems in opposite orders under two golds (Section 6.1), reporting the gold is part of reporting the measure. The decomposition of Section 4 applies to any object-, attribute- or relation-level score with reference-built gold, but where the sign changes is not predicted, and a score that removes the count confound and tracks human judgments better remains open.

## 8.5 Conclusion

Object-level hallucination scores respond to the gold and to the number of objects a caption names, not only to object choice. In a registered out-of-sample test on XM3600, and with a second Qwen backbone on 537 of the same 600 images, F1's verbosity slope is ordered by gold completeness.
