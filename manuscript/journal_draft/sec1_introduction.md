# 1 Introduction

Claims that a captioning model or a large vision-language model (LVLM) "hallucinates less" rest mostly on object-level scores. CHAIR (Rohrbach et al., 2018) counts mentioned objects that are absent from a gold set; object precision, recall and F1 compare the set of mentioned objects with the gold set; AMBER, THRONE and CCEval add coverage or class-wise F-scores. These scores are cheap, interpretable and easy to compare across papers. They are also measurements, and like any measurement they can respond to properties of the instrument rather than of the thing measured. This paper asks when an object-level score supports a comparison between two systems, and identifies three properties of the measurement that can decide the answer on their own.

**Count.** A caption that names fewer objects tends to have higher precision, because the first objects a model names are its most reliable ones. A method that shortens captions, stops earlier or prunes uncertain mentions therefore improves CHAIR without choosing objects better. The confound is known: POPE notes that CHAIR depends on caption length, CCEval balances mean length through prompting, and LeHaCE (Wei et al., NeurIPS 2024) fits a length–hallucination curve and evaluates at a fixed description length. What is missing in practice is a per-image control that holds the number of mentioned objects fixed and reports the difference that remains, with a paired interval. We report this as a specification curve rather than a single number. **Gold completeness.** Object metrics are scored against a gold set built from reference captions or from instance labels, and these golds differ in how many of the present objects they contain. A gold made from one reference caption names only the most salient objects; five references name more; instance labels name objects that no caption writer mentions. We show that the direction in which raw F1 rewards verbosity depends on this completeness. On 2,500 COCO images and 9 systems, with predictions frozen before the run, the slope of object F1 on the log number of mentioned objects is negative with one reference (−4.28 [−5.91, −2.45])<!-- src: .session_backup/2026-10-14/r11_verify/c1_b1_b6_reproduction.json :: B1_L1_mstar_and_stopping.check_coco_gold.refs1_randk.slope, slope_ci -->, positive with five (+11.08 [+9.76, +12.43])<!-- src: same :: check_coco_gold.refs5_randk.slope, slope_ci --> and larger with instance labels (+22.41 [+21.27, +23.48])<!-- src: same :: check_coco_gold.inst.slope, slope_ci -->. The same pair of systems can thus be ranked in opposite orders by the same metric under two golds. We explain this with a proposition (Section 4): if references are exchangeable and later mentions are less salient, the exponent of the precision–count frontier rises with the number of references, and the sign of F1's verbosity reward changes at most once, from negative to positive. The half-threshold rule behind the sign condition is due to Lipton et al. (2014), and the propensity view of human precision against the number of references is due to Chen et al. (2015, §4.2); what we add is the monotonicity in $k$, the single crossing, a split of the effect into two measurable channels, and tests on fixed outputs. The crossing point is not universal: on 20 public English LVLM outputs, which name more objects than short captions, the slope is still negative with five references and turns positive only under instance labels<!-- src: .session_backup/2026-10-14/r12/H3/out/n1_results.json :: families.F-B.gold.refs5_randk.slope, families.F-C.gold.refs5_randk.slope, families.F-C.gold.inst.slope -->. The sign is a property of the metric relative to the systems' verbosity, not a fixed fact about five-reference gold, and it is not a statement about what human judges prefer.

**Reference exchangeability.** The proposition, and any analysis that varies the number of references, assumes that the references of an image are exchangeable draws from independent writers. This is not always true. We give a simple diagnostic, the share of objects named by exactly two references that are named by two adjacent references, whose value under exchangeability is 0.40. Independent-annotator datasets sit at the null (COCO 0.402; UIT-ViIC 0.384)<!-- src: .session_backup/2026-10-14/r11_verify/c1_b1_b6_reproduction.json :: B2_L2_exchangeability.datasets.coco_en_test_coco80.adjacent_share, uitviic_vi_all_objects_in.adjacent_share -->; a single-writer corpus shows strong adjacency (companion paper). Where references are not exchangeable, reference-count curves, leave-one-reference-out "human ceilings" and species-richness estimates of completeness are within-writer quantities.

**Motivating case.** These three threats came to light together while evaluating Vietnamese captioning on KTVIC. There, the object-level gains we observed for selection and decoding methods vanished under at least one per-image count control, and whether a gain survived depended on which control was used; and the references turned out not to be exchangeable. We use KTVIC in this paper only as the motivating case and report no KTVIC result as a finding of this paper. The Vietnamese system, its controlled baseline, the pre-registered negative results and the full KTVIC audit (single writer, punctuation and matcher) are reported in a companion paper (Paper B, under review; TODO citation). The question this paper answers is general: what must be controlled, and what must be reported, before an object-level score can support a claim.

**Human validity.** A measurement-validity argument needs human judgments. On THumB 1.0 (Kasai et al., 2022) and the Composite set (Aditya et al., 2015), object precision agrees with expert precision judgments far more often under complete gold than under single-reference gold (pairwise agreement 0.736 to 0.903 on THumB; exploratory, not pre-registered)<!-- src: .session_backup/2026-10-14/r12/H1/pilot_results.json :: THumB.agree.negCHAIR|refs1|P|all.est, THumB.agree.negCHAIR|full|P|all.est -->, and object metrics agree with overall human judgments most on caption pairs that name the same number of objects. Humans in these sets are close to count-neutral (caption-level correlation of object count with the total score −0.05 on THumB)<!-- src: same :: THumB.corr_pearson_spearman.M|-|T -->. The same data also show a limit we state plainly: the count-controlled residual agrees with overall human preference less often than raw CHAIR does<!-- src: same :: THumB.P1b_acc(z->T)-acc(-CHAIR->T).est; Composite.P1b_acc(z->T)-acc(-CHAIR->T).est -->. Count control is therefore a condition for a valid comparison and a tool for attributing a difference, not a better quality score. **Contributions.**
1. A count-matched evaluation protocol with two per-image controls, a corpus-total match and a Shapley count–allocation–ranking decomposition, and a two-column reporting standard (raw score next to one pre-declared count-controlled score) (Section 3). Its application to 15 published decoding comparisons gives a specification curve from 2/15 to 6/15 surviving gains (Section 6); a count-matched leaderboard of 9 public training-based checkpoints is pending (TODO: N9).
2. A proposition on the gold-indexed precision–count frontier: monotone exponent, single crossing of the verbosity sign, and a b-channel / $\bar G$-channel split, with proof sketch and checks on fixed outputs (Section 4).
3. Tests of the verbosity sign across golds: confirmatory on COCO-2500, exploratory on English LVLM outputs and UIT-ViIC, and frozen predictions for XM3600 (Section 7; XM3600 TODO).
4. A reference-exchangeability diagnostic applied to five caption corpora, and its consequences for reference-count analyses (Section 5).
5. Human-validity evidence, stated narrowly (Section 8), and an audit of object-count imbalance in public hallucination-preference data (Section 9).

No claim in this paper is that a method beats the count frontier. The companion paper reports the methods we tried and their pre-registered outcomes.
