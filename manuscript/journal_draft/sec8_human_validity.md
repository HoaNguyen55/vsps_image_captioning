# 8 Experiments III: what human judgments support

Sections 6 and 7 are about properties of the metric. This section asks a narrower question: on existing human judgments, which of these properties line up with what people judge? We collected no new annotation and use no model-produced labels as evidence. Four public sets are used: THumB 1.0 (expert precision and recall ratings) and the Composite set (crowd correctness and thoroughness) for short COCO captions; Polaris-COCO (overall human ratings) at larger scale; and DOCCI-Critique (sentence-level factuality of long paragraphs from 14 VLMs). The human evidence is limited, and we state it as such: the central claims of the paper are properties of the metric (Sections 4–7), and nothing below shows that a count-controlled score is what humans want.

**Unit and statistic.** Unless stated otherwise, the unit is a pair of captions of the same image. For a score $X$ and a human judgment $H$, the agreement $\mathrm{acc}(X \to H)$ is the share of concordant pairs among pairs where both $X$ and $H$ differ; "count-differing" pairs name different numbers of COCO classes, "count-equal" pairs the same number. Objects are matched to COCO-80 with the CHAIR re-implementation of Section 6. Intervals are 2,000-draw image-bootstrap percentile intervals.

Three findings come out of the registered tests.

**The count-controlled residual is not a better quality score.** P1b failed in both sets, by a wide margin: the residual $z$ (true positives minus the frontier expectation at the caption's count) agrees with the human total less often than raw −CHAIR. Count control is an attribution tool, as Section 3.7 states, and the two-column standard keeps the raw score for that reason.

**Raw object precision tracks human precision judgments, and real errors are what humans penalize.** P1a held in both sets. On THumB, human precision falls by 1.16 points per real false positive and is unaffected by a mention that one reference omits but the full gold contains (P3). On the Composite set, whose ratings come from one crowd rater per item, such "pseudo-false positives" are penalized at about 40% of a real one, and P3 failed there.

**Humans are close to count-neutral, and full-gold F1 is not.** Humans prefer the longer caption in about half of the count-differing pairs (P2a), while F1's preference for the longer caption rises from 0.41 to 0.83 on THumB as the gold is completed (P2b). The human value lies closer to the one-reference value than to the full-gold value, and P2c's kill rule fired: completing the gold does not make F1 agree better with overall human judgments on count-differing pairs. The verbosity reward of Section 7 is therefore a property of the metric under a gold; we do not claim that complete-gold F1's preference for longer captions is what human judges want.

**Exploratory (not registered).** Two patterns were not predicted and are reported as exploratory. First, gold completeness is the largest single driver of agreement between −CHAIR and human precision on THumB: 0.736 with one reference, 0.797 with four, 0.903 with full gold<!-- src: .session_backup/2026-10-14/r12/H1/pilot_results.json :: THumB.agree.negCHAIR|{refs1,refs4,full}|P|all.est -->; against the human total the values are 0.627, 0.714, 0.793 on THumB and 0.743, 0.838, 0.873 on Composite<!-- src: same :: {THumB,Composite}.agree.negCHAIR|{refs1,refs4,full}|T|all.est -->. Second, −CHAIR under full gold agrees with the human total more on count-equal than on count-differing pairs: 0.846 versus 0.773 on THumB and 0.946 versus 0.838 on Composite<!-- src: same :: {THumB,Composite}.agree.negCHAIR|full|T|{ceq,cdiff}.est -->. Polaris (Section 8.2) tests both patterns as registered predictions.

It is the only set in this section with long, LVLM-style outputs. The registered matcher is COCO-80 (the CHAIR matcher used throughout)<!-- src: same :: registered_G3.matcher = COCO-80_E1_CHAIR_registered -->. The registered verdict (G3) is **MIXED**<!-- src: same :: registered_G3.verdict -->.

**G3-(i), within-VLM count effect: primary fails.** The prediction was that, within each VLM, paragraphs that name more objects receive lower human factuality, required on three clauses: mean within-VLM slope < 0, median within-VLM slope < 0, and a pooled VLM-fixed-effects slope with interval below 0. Under the primary human measure (sentence-level majority), the mean slope is −0.0127 [−0.0265, +0.0007] and the pooled slope −0.0157 [−0.0286, −0.0024], but the median within-VLM slope is +0.000371, with 7 of 14 VLMs negative; the median clause fails, so G3-(i) primary fails<!-- src: same :: registered_G3.G3_i.P_human_sent_maj.{mean_within_vlm_slope,mean_within_vlm_slope_ci_95,beta_FE,beta_FE_ci_95,median_within_vlm_slope,n_vlms_negative_slope,failed_clause,pass} -->. **G3-(ii), ranking the 14 VLMs: point estimate passes, interval includes 0.** The prediction was that the count-controlled (matched-prefix) object score ranks the 14 VLMs closer to human factuality than raw object precision does, by at least +0.10 in Kendall's $\tau$. ## 8.5 What the human evidence supports

On short COCO captions (THumB, Composite, Polaris), human judgments support two conditions for a valid object-level comparison: complete gold, and matched count. Agreement between object metrics and humans is higher under complete gold (registered on Polaris), and it is highest on pairs that name the same number of objects (registered on Polaris; exploratory on THumB and Composite). The same data do not support the count-controlled residual as a quality score (P1b failed twice), and they show humans to be close to count-neutral, so neither the one-reference penalty nor the complete-gold reward for verbosity is a human preference.

On long paragraphs (DOCCI-Critique), the registered result is mixed: the within-VLM count effect fails on its primary measure, and the ranking advantage of the count-controlled score is above threshold but not distinguishable from 0. The evidence for count control on long LVLM outputs is therefore weak, and the registered matcher measures only a small part of these outputs. We present the human results as a check on the measurement argument, not as its foundation.
