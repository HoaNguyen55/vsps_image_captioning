**Object hallucination metrics.** CHAIR (Rohrbach et al., 2018) scores the share of mentioned COCO classes absent from a gold built from instance labels and reference captions, at the instance and sentence level. It is precision-type: a caption that names fewer objects can lower it without choosing objects better. POPE (Li et al., 2023; arXiv 2305.10355) replaces free-form scoring with yes/no probing and notes that CHAIR depends on the instruction and the caption length. HallE-Control and its CCEval benchmark (Zhai et al., 2023; arXiv 2310.01779) balance mean caption length and object count across models through prompting; the balancing acts on means and, by the authors' account, is not strictly enforced (arXiv v3, Appendix A.4). AMBER (Wang et al., 2023; arXiv 2311.07397) reports a coverage score (recall of annotated objects) next to its hallucination rate. THRONE (Kaul et al., CVPR 2024; arXiv 2405.05256) evaluates free-form generations with class-wise precision, recall and an F-score that weights precision, using the class-wise F0.5-score as its main metric (§3.1). ALOHa (Petryk et al., 2024; arXiv 2404.02904) extends the vocabulary beyond COCO-80. All of these define what is counted; none fixes the number of mentioned objects per image when two systems are compared.

**Length and count as confounds.** LeHaCE (Wei, Wang, Zhang, Fan and Zhao, NeurIPS 2024; DOI 10.52202/079017-3538) is the closest prior work in method. It fits a model-specific curve of hallucination against description length, evaluates every model at a uniform length, and uses the curve's slope as a stability measure. Our controls differ in three ways: they match the number of mentioned objects per image exactly rather than through a fitted curve over word length; they come with paired, image-clustered intervals and a decomposition of the difference; and they are applied to other groups' released outputs. We do not claim that object count is a better control unit than length for English LVLMs; our registered test of that claim failed (Section 6). SumGD (Min et al., Findings of NAACL 2025; DOI 10.18653/v1/2025.findings-naacl.235) remarks that CHAIR can be lowered by shorter captions and reports recall next to it; "Less is More" (Yue et al., 2024; arXiv 2402.14545) shortens outputs by encouraging the end-of-sequence token and treats the recall loss as an acceptable trade-off. Outside captioning, Sun, Shapira, Dagan and Nenkova (2019; ACL Anthology W19-2303) show that ROUGE recall rises and precision falls with summary length, so that ROUGE F1 first rises and then declines, and normalize a system's ROUGE F1 by that of a random system with the same average output length; this is the nearest precedent for count matching. Napoles, Van Durme and Callison-Burch (2011; ACL Anthology W11-1611) show that compression rate predicts the judged quality of sentence compressions and argue that compressors be compared at similar compression rates. Closest in aim, Fazli, Mansouri, Marvania and Zhu (2026; arXiv 2609.01888) re-evaluate six inference-time mitigation methods (VCD, M3ID, AGLA, CAAC, CEI, AFTER) on three 7B LVLMs (LLaVA-1.5, LLaVA-NeXT, InstructBLIP) with CHAIR and AMBER paired with object recall and coverage, plus MMStar for general capability, and conclude that "current evaluation protocols may overestimate progress by rewarding conservative generation" (abstract). Their evidence is aggregate: hallucination and informativeness metrics are correlated across model–method configurations (e.g. CHAIR$_s$ and object recall, Pearson $r = 0.73$, their §3). On our reading of v1, they do not match object count per image, do not vary the completeness of the gold (CHAIR is scored against COCO annotations and AMBER against its own object lists), and do not report how conclusions change across alternative controls. (Their abstract counts four benchmarks; the body evaluates CHAIR, AMBER and MMStar.) Sandhu, Senapati and Kammachi (2026; arXiv 2608.24966; Actionable Interpretability Workshop, COLM 2026) lower CHAIR$_i$ on LLaVA-1.5 with a head-targeted intervention, show that the reduction persists under fixed decoding budgets of 64 and 128 tokens, and report that the method stays "shorter and more conservative" with object recall falling from 0.78 to 0.70; this is a token-budget control, not an object-count control. On the training side, Long et al. (2026; arXiv 2609.35294) find that on-policy RL for hallucination falls into a shortcut of "saying less" and build a densely annotated presence/absence gold (DOPA) so that valid claims can be rewarded; this is the training-time counterpart of our gold-completeness argument, without a sign analysis. Nguyen et al. (2026; arXiv 2610.14976; IEEE Access per arXiv comment) report reward hacking when RL optimizes NLP metrics for hallucination and add an object-coverage term to the reward. We take the concern from these works and contribute a pre-registered per-image measurement of it.

**Thresholds, F-measures and incomplete gold.** Lipton, Elkan and Narayanaswamy (2014; arXiv 1402.1892; ECML-PKDD 2014 as "Optimal Thresholding of Classifiers to Maximize F1 Measure", LNCS 8725, pp. 225–239, DOI 10.1007/978-3-662-44851-9_15) prove that the F1-optimal decision rule adds an item when its probability of being correct exceeds half the attainable F1. The sign condition in our Section 4 is their result applied to object mentions; we cite it and do not claim it. Chen et al. (2015; arXiv 1504.00325, §4.2) model human caption precision as a function of the number of references with one naming probability per word and no image-level salience; our propensity model generalizes their §4.2 by letting naming propensity vary within an image. Positive–unlabeled evaluation (Jain, Prabhu and Varma, 2016) shows that missing labels bias metrics toward head labels; F-measures are estimable from positive–unlabeled data only under the selected-completely-at-random (SCAR) assumption (Saunders and Freitas, 2022; arXiv 2206.02423). Our b-channel is the part of the verbosity effect that SCAR missingness cannot produce. In information retrieval, incomplete relevance judgments bias evaluation measures (Buckley and Voorhees, 2004; DOI 10.1145/1008992.1009000), and recall-oriented measures vary with pool depth (Lu, Moffat and Culpepper, 2016; Information Retrieval Journal 19:416–445; DOI 10.1007/s10791-016-9282-6). Incidence-based species accumulation and rarefaction (Colwell et al., 2012; Chao et al., 2014, DOI 10.1890/13-0133.1) supply the curve $U(k)$ we use for expected gold size.

**Gold quality and rater equivalence.** ImageNet ReaL (Beyer et al., 2020; arXiv 2006.07159) re-labels the ImageNet validation set with multiple labels per image and shows that model rankings change when the gold is made more complete; RePOPE (Neuhaus and Hein, 2025; arXiv 2504.15707) revisits POPE's labels in the same spirit. Resnick, Kong, Schoenebeck and Weninger (arXiv 2106.01254, v3 2026, "Principled Evaluation with Human Labels: One Rater at a Time and Rater Equivalence") define rater (survey) equivalence: the smallest number of human raters whose combined labels match a classifier, scored one held-out rater at a time. That unit presupposes independent raters; Section 5 shows a caption dataset where the references of an image are not independent and the unit is undefined.

**Caption evaluation against human judgments.** THumB 1.0 (Kasai et al., NAACL 2022; arXiv 2111.08940) provides expert precision and recall ratings for COCO captions of four systems and a human reference; the Composite set (Aditya et al., 2015; arXiv 1511.03292) provides crowd correctness and thoroughness ratings; Polaris (Wada, Kaneda, Saito and Sugiura, CVPR 2024; arXiv 2402.18091, introduced with the Polos metric) provides 131K human judgments from 550 evaluators; DOCCI-Critique (Gordon et al.; arXiv 2506.07631) provides sentence-level factuality ratings for long paragraphs from 14 VLMs. Berger et al. (TACL 2025; arXiv 2408.04909) survey caption-evaluation metrics and their validity. We use these sets only to test what object metrics agree with, not to propose a new learned metric. For long and detailed captions, CapArena (Cheng et al., Findings of ACL 2025; arXiv 2503.12329) collects over 6,000 human pairwise battles and reports that metric biases can change model rankings; CAPability (Liu et al., NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks; arXiv 2502.14914) scores caption precision and recall per visual dimension; VELA and its LongCap-Arena benchmark (Matsuda et al., EMNLP 2025 per arXiv comment; arXiv 2509.25818) provide human judgments of descriptiveness, relevance and fluency for long captions; CapMAS (Lee et al., ICML 2025 per arXiv comment; arXiv 2412.15484) proposes separate factuality and coverage metrics for hyper-detailed captions. These benchmarks separate correctness from coverage; none matches the number of mentioned objects between the captions compared. VaPR (Wadhawan et al., 2025; arXiv 2510.01700) reports stylistic and length biases in synthetic LVLM preference data and builds length- and style-matched hard negatives; Section 9 builds on this and measures the object-count channel, which length matching need not remove.

**Vietnamese captioning data.** UIT-ViIC (Lam et al., 2020; arXiv 2002.00175) provides Vietnamese captions for sports images drawn from COCO, written by independent annotators. KTVIC (Pham, Nguyen, Vuong and Ha, 2024; arXiv 2401.08100) covers the life domain; its annotation interface asks one annotator to enter five captions per image, with three annotators in total and a revision pass by two researchers (§3.2, Fig. 5). UIT-OpenViIC (Bui, Nguyen and Nguyen, Signal Processing: Image Communication 140:117430, 2026; DOI 10.1016/j.image.2025.117430) is a larger Vietnamese benchmark; we use it only through aggregate statistics (Section 11). Vietnamese-specific measurement issues (word segmentation, tone marks, classifier nouns, punctuation conventions) and the KTVIC audit are treated in the companion paper.

**Reporting standards.** sacreBLEU (Post, 2018; arXiv 1804.08771) made the parameters of BLEU (tokenization, number of references, brevity penalty) explicit and reportable through a version signature. SacreEOS (Hu, Cavicchioli and Capotondi, ICIAP 2023, LNCS, pp. 39–50; DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-43148-7_4; arXiv 2305.12254) does the same for the end-of-sequence convention in self-critical captioning training. Our two-column standard is in this tradition: it does not change any metric, it fixes what has to be reported next to it.
